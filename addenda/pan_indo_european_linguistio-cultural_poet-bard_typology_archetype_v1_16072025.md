# **The Indo-European Bardic Figure: Spatio-Temporal Survey of the *kuśīlava* and Cognates**

## I. Proto-Indo-European Origins and Early Diffusion

**Chronological Anchor:** *c.* 2500–1500 BCE  
**Spatio-Cultural Zone:** Pontic-Caspian Steppe to Bactria–Margiana, moving west into Europe and east into South Asia and Iran via Indo-Aryan and Indo-Iranian migration.

### 1. Proto-Root: *ḱlew- / *ḱlewos*  
- Meaning: “to hear, fame, renown”  
- Reflexes:
  - **Greek:** κλέος (*kleos*) – fame  
  - **Sanskrit:** श्रवस् (*śravas*) – fame  
  - **Latin:** cluere → *inclutus* (famous)  
- Likely lexical core for early poet-figures: the one who “spreads fame through recitation”

**Function:**  
- Vocal memory transmission; reciter of heroic gesta  
- Bonded to chieftains/kings, but potentially liminal/itinerant

## II. South Asia: From Vedic to Epic Period (1500 BCE – 200 CE)

### **A. Vedic Period (1500–600 BCE)**  
**Region:** Punjab to Western Gangetic plain (Ṛgvedic territory)  
**Primary Figure:** **कवि (*kaví*)**  
- **Script:** देवनागरी  
- **IAST:** *kaví*  
- **Phonemic:** [kəʋiː]  
- **Meaning:** “seer-poet”  
- Gendered connotation: divine access, inspired speech  

**Textual Anchors:**  
- Ṛgveda: the *kaví* is a ṛṣi but also composer-performer  
- Not yet professionalised or differentiated into classes

### **B. Later Vedic / Epics and Early Sūtra Period (600 BCE – 200 CE)**  
**Regions:** Greater Magadha, Kosala, Kuru-Pañcāla, early Janapadas  
**Key Roles Introduced:**

#### 1. **Sūta (सूतः)**  
- Originally: charioteer → later: bard/genealogist  
- Attested: *Mahābhārata*, *Rāmāyaṇa*, early Purāṇas  
- Dual varṇa affiliation: possible Kṣatriya–Brāhmaṇa blend  

#### 2. **Māgadha (मागधः)**  
- Region: Magadha (modern Bihar), culturally influential by 6th century BCE  
- Role: Professional bard, especially genealogist and panegyrist  
- **Attestation:** *Mahābhārata*, *Nāṭyaśāstra*  
- Stigma develops in normative Dharmaśāstra texts—seen as outcaste or low status

#### 3. **Kuśīlava (कुशीलवः)**  
- First attestation: *Rāmāyaṇa: Uttarakāṇḍa*  
- Fictionalized performers: **Kuśa and Lava**, sons of Rāma reciting his own epic
- Function: bard, actor, rhapsode
- **Nāṭyaśāstra** (c. 1st–2nd c. CE): *kuśīlava* designates performers in early theater; they specialise in **nāṭya** (drama), **gāna** (song), **vāditra** (instrument), and **nṛtta** (dance), suggesting **ritual-professionalization**.

## III. Europe: Greco-Roman and Celtic Worlds (1200 BCE – 500 CE)

### **A. Ancient Greece**  
**Region:** Mycenae, later Pan-Hellenic Greece  
- **ἀοιδός (*aoidós*)**: singer-bard (Homer identified as such)  
- **ποιητής (*poiētḗs*)**: “maker, poet,” formal composer  
- Role: Ritual praise, elite feasts, public festivities  
- **Texts:** Homeric epics (c. 8th BCE), Hesiod  
- Parallels: Rhapsodes ("song-stitchers") with itinerant bardic functions

### **B. Gaulish and Insular Celtic (Hallstatt to Sub-Roman Period)**  
**Region:** Gaul, Britannia, Hibernia  
- **Proto-Celtic:** *bardos* → Old Irish *bard*, **‘praise-poet’**  
- **Role:** Praise-keeper, ritual satirist, genealogist, historian  
- Societal Status: Elite, often protected by law  
- **Textual/Legal Attestation:** Early Irish law tracts, *Tochmarc Emire*, *Lebor na hUidre*

## IV. Central and Northern Europe: Germanic Worlds (1st–10th c. CE)

### **A. Old English and Norse**  
**Figures:**  
- **Scōp (Old English):** composed and recited heroic verse; *Widsith* (9th c.)  
- **Skald (Old Norse):** court poet; attested via Icelandic sagas, runic inscriptions  
- Function: Negotiation of fame, propaganda, royal legitimation  
- **Etymology:** *skald* from Proto-Germanic *skallōną*, “to resound”; akin to Indo-Iranian *śru* ‘to hear’

**Temporal Frame:**  
- Skaldic poetry flourished in 9th–13th centuries CE  
- Transition post-Christianisation: from oral courtier to literate chronicler

## V. Classical and Early Medieval South Asia (200–1100 CE)

### **Regional Centres:**  
- **Nāṭyaśāstra tradition**: Kātyāyana, Bhārata  
- **Courtly centres:** Ujjayinī, Kāñcī, Mālava, Kāśmīra

### **Medieval Transformation:**
- Sanskrit traditions:
  - In **Nāṭyaśāstra**, performance becomes **codified**, emplacing *kuśīlavas* as formal actors and musicians (high artisan-artistic status, yet **ritually marginal** in Dharmaśāstra).
  - They coalesce into tribal/peripheral artisan guilds or **śailūṣas** (शैलूष) in later dramaturgical lexicons.

- Jain and Buddhist refractions:
  - Jain court drama (*Rūpaka*), monks describe and sometimes interact with performers (*nāṭyadhārins*), indicating continued interaction with bardic classes.
  - In narrative literature such as **Kathāsaritsāgara** (c. 11th CE), traveling bards perform heroic tales—continuity of function with variation in caste or religious roles.

## VII. Conclusion: Indo-European Continuum and Regional Inflection

The **kuśīlava**, descending from Indo-European bardic roots, becomes emblematic of a wider caste of **ritually trained oral specialists** serving courts, temples, and assemblies across the Indo-European cultural space. A **universal pattern** emerges, defined by:

- Ancestral fame-narration
- Royal affiliation through patronage (or clerical redefinition)
- Oral-aural performance as politico-ritual capital
- A **late structural bifurcation**: sanctified literary poet vs. profane performer
