# Bhagavan Sri Panduranga #

# Spatio-Temporal Survey of the Holy Name Panduranga

## Etymology and Earliest Attestations

The sacred name **Panduranga** derives from Sanskrit पाण्डुरङ्ग (Pāṇḍuraṅga), composed of two elements: "**Pāṇḍu**" meaning white or pale, and "**Raṅga**" meaning color or complexion[1][2][3]. Thus, Panduranga etymologically signifies "**one with white or pale complexion**," though paradoxically, the deity is traditionally depicted with a **dark complexion**[2][3], creating a fascinating semantic tension that has persisted throughout history.

The earliest documented reference to Panduranga worship appears in a **Rashtrakuta copper plate inscription dated 516 CE**[4][5], establishing the antiquity of this divine name. According to inscriptional evidence, the place was called "**Pandaraga Vittaga**" in copper plates found in Shimoga, Hassan, and Kadur dating back to **618 CE**, which later evolved into Panduranga Vitthala[5]. An inscription mentioning Vithoba as "**Panduranga Vitthala**" dates to **1270 CE**[5 Distribution and Linguistic Variants

### Primary Seat: Maharashtra

The epicenter of Panduranga worship remains **Pandharpur, Maharashtra**, located on the banks of the **Chandrabhaga River** (locally known as Bhima)[4][6][7]. The town is referred to by various names in ancient texts: **Pandhari, Pandurangapur, Phaganipur, and Pundarik Kshetra**[6]. The area is revered as **"Bhu-vaikuntha"** (the spiritual world on earth) and **"Dakshina Dvaraka"** (the Dvaraka of the South)[6].

### Karnataka Extension

The name Panduranga found significant resonance in Karnataka, particularly through the **Haridasa tradition**. The famous **Vittala Temple at Hampi** was dedicated to **Lord Vittala** (another name for Panduranga) and was built during the reign of **King Krishna Deva Raya** around **1513 CE**[8][9]. The temple was constructed as **"Vijaya Vitthala Temple"** after the king's victory over Pratapa Rudra Gajapati[9].

### Southeast Asian Expansion

Remarkably, the name Panduranga transcended Indian borders and became geographically embodied in **Southeast Asia**. **Panduranga** (Old Cham: *Paṅrauṅ* / *Panrāṅ*; Sanskrit: *Pāṇḍuraṅga*) was a **Cham Principality** in present-day south-central Vietnam, centered around the modern city of **Phan Rang**[10][11][12]. This principality existed from **757-1693 CE** and represented the **rump state of the Champa kingdom** after its destruction in 1471[10][13]. The name's adoption in this distant land demonstrates the extensive cultural and religious influence of Panduranga worship across maritime Southeast Asia.

## Scriptural and Literary References

### Puranic Literature

The name Panduranga appears in several **Puranic texts**. The **Panduranga Mahatmya** is found in the **Skanda Purana**, **Padma Purana**, and **Vishnu Purana**[14][15]. These texts detail the legends associated with the deity and establish the theological framework for worship.

### Sanskrit Stotra Literature

The **Pandurangashtakam**, attributed to **Adi Shankaracharya** (8th century CE), stands as one of the earliest and most significant Sanskrit compositions dedicated to Panduranga[6][16][17][18]. This eight-verse hymn establishes that Vithoba worship existed as early as the **9th century**[19], though the attribution to Shankaracharya remains contested by some scholars.

### Varkari Literary Tradition

The **Varkari tradition** produced an extensive corpus of devotional literature in **Marathi** centered on Panduranga:

**Sant Jnaneshwar** (1275-1296 CE) laid the foundation of Panduranga-centered bhakti through his **Jnaneshwari** (1290 CE) and other works[20][21]. His **Tirthavali Gatha** mentions pilgrimage traditions associated with Panduranga[22].

**Sant Namdev** (traditionally c. 1270-1350 CE) composed numerous **abhangas** (devotional poems) in praise of Panduranga[23][24][25]. His compositions, along with those of **Sant Tukaram** (17th century), form the backbone of Varkari devotional literature[23][26].

The **abhanga** tradition represents a unique literary form—**flawless divine poems** that flow continuously without interruption[23][27][24]. These compositions were sung during the famous **Varkari pilgrimages** to Pandharpur and became integral to the spiritual and cultural identity of Maharashtra.

## Haridasa Tradition in Karnataka

The Karnataka **Haridasa movement** developed its own rich tradition of Panduranga devotion through the **Dasa Sahitya**. **Purandara Dasa** (1484-1564 CE), known as the "**Father of Karnataka Music**," composed devotional songs with the signature "**Purandara Vittala**"[28][29][30]. His contemporary **Kanaka Dasa** and other Haridasas created thousands of compositions (suladis, keertanas, devaranamas) celebrating Vittala-Panduranga[31][32].

## Iconographic Distinctiveness

The iconography of Panduranga presents several unique features that distinguish this form from other manifestations of Vishnu:

**Arms Akimbo Posture**: The most distinctive feature is Panduranga's stance with **hands placed on hips** (arms akimbo), suggesting a relaxed, intimate relationship with devotees[33][34][35].

**Standing on a Brick**: The deity stands upon a **brick** (Marathi: *vit*), which gives rise to the name **Vitthala** - "one standing on a brick"[36][33][2]. This iconographic element is directly linked to the **Pundalik legend**.

**Iconic Attributes**: Panduranga holds a **shankha** (conch) in the left hand and **chakra** (discus) in the right hand, traditional Vishnu symbols[34]. He wears a **tulsi-bead necklace** with the **kaustubha gem** and **makara-kundala** (fish-shaped earrings)[34].

**Solitary Presence**: Unlike many Vishnu temples, Panduranga appears **alone** without his consort in the main sanctum, though **Rukmini** is present in a separate shrine[2][3].

## Regional Script Variations

The name Panduranga appears across multiple scripts throughout its geographical spread:

- **Devanagari**: पाण्डुरङ्ग (Sanskrit/Marathi)
- **Kannada**: ಪಾಂಡುರಂಗ (Karnataka Haridasa literature)
- **Telugu**: పాండురంగ (Telugu-speaking regions)
- **Old Cham Script**: *Paṅrauṅ* / *Panrāṅ* (Vietnam Champa inscriptions)
- **Tamil**: பாண்டுரங்க (Tamil literary references)

## Oral Traditions and Folk Culture

### Varkari Oral Tradition

The **Varkari sampradaya** maintained rich oral traditions through:

**Nama Japam**: Continuous recitation of Panduranga's names during pilgrimages[23][24].

**Folk Songs**: Housewives in Pandharpur traditionally sang **abhanga songs** while performing household chores[23][24].

**Pilgrimage Songs**: The **Varkari pilgrimages** to Pandharpur feature continuous singing of traditional songs, creating a mobile oral tradition spanning centuries[22][37].

### Musical Traditions

Classical musicians like **Bhimsen Joshi** and **Suresh Wadkar** have contributed to preserving and popularizing Panduranga devotional music by adding sophisticated musical arrangements to traditional abhangas[23][24].

## Temples and Sacred Geography

### Primary Temple: Pandharpur

The **Shri Vitthal-Rukmini Mandir** at Pandharpur, built by **King Vishnuvardhana** of the **Hoysala Empire** between **1108-1152 CE**[38][7], houses the primary **black basalt idol** of Panduranga, measuring **3 feet 9 inches** in height[34]. The temple follows the **Hemadpanthi architectural style** and has been renovated multiple times throughout history[36].

### Karnataka Temples

The **Vittala Temple Complex at Hampi** represents the architectural pinnacle of Panduranga worship in Karnataka[8][9][39][40]. This **UNESCO World Heritage Site** showcases the sophistication of **Vijayanagara temple architecture** and includes the famous **stone chariot**.

### Regional Temples

Historical records indicate that **multiple temples dedicated to Vittala** were constructed along the **Tungabhadra river banks** by the **12th century CE**[5], demonstrating the widespread geographical expansion of Panduranga worship.

## Lines of Transmission and Dissemination

### Northern Transmission

**Sant Namdev's** travels to North India facilitated the transmission of Panduranga worship beyond Maharashtra. His compositions are included in the **Guru Granth Sahib**, establishing connections with **Sikh tradition**[25].

### Southern Transmission

The **Karnataka Haridasa movement** represented an independent but parallel development of Panduranga devotion, creating its own theological and literary traditions while maintaining core iconographic elements.

### Maritime Transmission

The establishment of **Panduranga principality in Vietnam** suggests **maritime transmission** through **Cham-Hindu cultural networks**, demonstrating the deity's influence across the **Indian Ocean cultural sphere**.

## Contemporary Traditions

The **Varkari tradition** continues to thrive with **millions of pilgrims** participating in biannual pilgrimages to Pandharpur during **Ashadhi Ekadashi** and **Kartiki Ekadashi**[7][14]. The tradition maintains its egalitarian character, welcoming devotees regardless of caste or social status[5].

The name Panduranga thus represents not merely a theological concept but a **living cultural phenomenon** that has shaped religious, literary, artistic, and social traditions across vast geographical and temporal spans. Its unique iconography, combined with its extensive literary corpus and continuing oral traditions, establishes Panduranga as one of the most culturally significant divine names in the Hindu tradition, with influence extending far beyond the boundaries of the Indian subcontinent.
