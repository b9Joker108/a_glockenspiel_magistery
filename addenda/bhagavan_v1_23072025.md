Does another nearby verse in context, state this definition is of Bhagavan?

Yes, the verse **explicitly defines what “Bhagavan” means** and is **presented in direct response to a question asking for this definition** within its textual context in the Vishnu Purana[1][3][5][6][7]. The immediate context of Vishnu Purana 6.5 includes a discussion in which Sage Parashara describes the supreme qualities or opulences (bhaga) that, when possessed in fullness, constitute the nature of *Bhagavan*.

The verse itself:

**ऐश्वर्यस्य समग्रस्य वीर्यस्य यशसः श्रियः।  
ज्ञान-वैराग्ययोश्चैव षण्णां भग इतीरणा॥**  
(*aiśvaryasya samagrasya vīryasya yaśasaḥ śriyaḥ,  
jñāna-vairāgyayoś caiva ṣaṇṇāṁ bhaga itīṅganā*)  

states that “**bhaga**” is the collective term for these six opulences, and **the one who possesses them in full is called "Bhagavan"**. The term “bhagavān iti” (भगवान् इति) means “called Bhagavan” or "so-called Bhagavan" in this context[1][3][5][6][7].

Multiple reputable summaries, translations, and commentaries—including those by Swami Dayananda, the Hare Krishna tradition, and academic translations—confirm this reading. They note that **the text is making a precise definitional statement**: anyone—or, as emphasized, only the *Supreme Person*—who possesses all six opulences entirely is designated as "Bhagavan"[1][3][5][6][7].

Further, many sources reinforce that this is the **authoritative scriptural definition of "Bhagavan"**, not merely a general description or a poetic attribution[1][3][5][6][7]. The verse is referenced in teachings and lectures as *the* definition, and often cited with this explicit context.

Therefore, **the context and the structure of the verse itself make it clear that it is a direct, categorical definition of “Bhagavan” as per Sage Parashara in Vishnu Purana 6.5.47**[1][3][5][6][7].

What verse says in Vishnu Purana states it is about Bhagavan?

The verse **Vishnu Purana 6.5.47** itself states the definition of “Bhagavan.” The verse uses the phrase **"षण्णां भग इतीङ्गना"** (*ṣaṇṇāṁ bhaga iti-iṅganā*), which is translated as “these six are called *bhaga*,” and by linguistic extension, *one who possesses them is called Bhagavan*.

Specifically, the term "इतीङ्गना" (iti-iṅganā) in the verse literally means "this is the definition" or "this is called." Commentaries and translations consistently affirm this contextual meaning—for example:

- "The term ‘Bhagavan’ or God, is thus defined by Parasara Muni as one who is full in six opulences" (citing Vishnu Purana 6.5.47)[1][4][5][6][8].

- "Bhagavan means He who possesses these six opulences in full..." (again, specifying the verse)[3][6].

Thus, **Vishnu Purana 6.5.47 is the verse that both gives the list of opulences and states this is the definition of "Bhagavan"**[1][3][4][5][6]. No separate verse is required, as the definition and its attribution to "Bhagavan" are given in the same verse.

If you are seeking a precise verse where it says "this is the definition of Bhagavan", this is the verse (6.5.47)—the last phrase "षण्णां भग इतीङ्गना" makes exactly that statement[1][3][5][6].

# The Definition of Bhagavan: A Detailed Survey of Parashara's Verse

## The Sanskrit Verse and Its Attribution

The verse you have presented is indeed attributed to **Maharshi Parashara** and is found in the **Vishnu Purana 6.5.47**[1][2][3]. The exact Sanskrit text, as consistently cited across multiple sources, reads:

**ऐश्वर्यस्य समग्रस्य वीर्यस्य यशसः श्रियः**  
**ज्ञान-वैराग्ययोश्चैव षण्णां भग इतीङ्गना**

*aiśvaryasya samagrasya vīryasya yaśasaḥ śriyaḥ*  
*jñāna-vairāgyayoś caiva ṣaṇṇāṁ bhaga itīṅganā*

Note that you mentioned a variant reading with "धर्मस्य (vīryasya)" in parentheses, but the standard and widely accepted reading across all scholarly sources is **vīryasya** (वीर्यस्य), meaning "strength" or "power"[1][2][4][3].

## The Author: Maharshi Parashara

**Maharshi Parashara** holds an extraordinary position in Vedic literature as both a sage and the author of numerous foundational texts[5][6]. He was the **son of Shakti Muni and Adrishyanti**, but was raised by his grandfather, the renowned **Sage Vasishtha**, one of the Saptarishis (Seven Great Seers)[5][6].

Parashara is credited as the **author of the Vishnu Purana**, which is considered the first among the eighteen Mahapuranas[2][7]. He is also revered as the **father of Jyotish Shastra (Vedic Astrology)**[5][8] and is the father of **Veda Vyasa**, who later compiled the Vedas and authored the Mahabharata[7][5].

The sage's authority in defining divine attributes is unquestionable, as he received divine knowledge directly from **Sage Pulastya** after being dissuaded from completing a destructive sacrifice against demons[6][9]. This blessing granted him comprehensive understanding of scriptures and the ability to compose the Puranas[6].

## The Definition of Bhagavan: The Six Opulences

This verse provides the **definitive Sanskrit etymology and theological definition of the term "Bhagavan"**[1][2][4]. According to Parashara's definition, **Bhagavan** refers to one who possesses **six divine opulences (ṣaṇṇāṁ bhaga)** in their complete and perfect form[1][2][10]:

### The Six Opulences (Bhaga) Explained

1. **Aiśvarya (ऐश्वर्य)** - Complete sovereignty, unlimited wealth, or absolute lordship[11][4][12]. This represents supreme authority and dominion over all creation.

2. **Vīrya (वीर्य)** - Complete strength, power, or prowess[4][12][10]. This encompasses unlimited physical and spiritual power.

3. **Yaśas (यशस्)** - Complete fame, glory, or renown[11][4][12]. This represents universal recognition and eternal glory.

4. **Śrī (श्री)** - Complete beauty, splendor, or prosperity[11][4][12]. This includes both material prosperity and divine radiance.

5. **Jñāna (ज्ञान)** - Complete knowledge or omniscience[11][4][12]. This represents all-encompassing wisdom and understanding.

6. **Vairāgya (वैराग्य)** - Complete renunciation or detachment[11][4][12]. This signifies perfect dispassion despite possessing all other opulences.

## Philosophical and Theological Significance

### Etymology of Bhagavan

The word **Bhagavan** is derived from the Sanskrit root **bhaj** (भज्), meaning "to share, distribute, partake, or revere"[13][14]. The term **bhaga** represents these six opulences, and the suffix **-van** (or **-vat**) means "one who possesses"[2][15]. Thus, **Bhagavan** literally means "one who possesses bhaga" - the six divine opulences in their entirety[1][2][10].

### Theological Implications

This definition establishes a **concrete, measurable standard for divinity** rather than abstract philosophical speculation[2][3]. It provides specific criteria by which true divinity can be recognized and distinguished from partial manifestations of power or beauty[1][2].

The verse emphasizes that these qualities must be possessed **samagra** (समग्र) - completely or in full[1][2][10]. This distinguishes the Supreme from any individual who might possess some of these qualities partially, as many powerful, beautiful, or wise beings do[15][7].

### Universal Application

Significantly, this definition is not sectarian but provides a **universal framework for understanding divinity**[11][12]. While most commonly applied to Krishna and Vishnu in Vaishnavism, the term has been used across Hindu traditions, and the underlying concept appears in other Indian religions[11][16][17].

In Buddhist literature, Buddha is also referred to as **Bhagavan** or **Bhagavā**, where it carries similar connotations of being "Blessed One" or "Lord"[16][17][14].

## Historical Context and Transmission

The **Vishnu Purana** represents one of the earliest and most authoritative Puranic texts[18][19]. According to traditional accounts, this wisdom was originally imparted by **Brahma** to various sages, then transmitted through a lineage including **Daksha**, **Purukutsa**, **Sarasvata**, and finally to **Parashara**[9][19].

The verse appears in Book 6, Chapter 5, verse 47 of the Vishnu Purana, within a broader discussion of divine attributes and cosmological principles[1][2]. This placement within the text demonstrates its fundamental importance to understanding the nature of the Supreme Reality.

## Contemporary Relevance

This ancient definition continues to influence contemporary Hindu theology and practice[15][7]. It provides a framework for understanding how divinity manifests in the world and offers devotees specific qualities to contemplate and aspire toward[20][15].

The concept has particular significance in **Bhakti traditions**, where devotees seek to develop loving relationships with deities who embody these perfect qualities[7][14]. It also provides philosophical grounding for understanding the difference between the Supreme Reality and its various manifestations or incarnations[20][21].

## Conclusion

Parashara's verse stands as a **masterpiece of theological precision**, offering both a linguistic etymology and a practical definition of divine perfection[2][10]. Its enduring influence across centuries of Hindu thought testifies to its profound insight into the nature of the Supreme Reality. By establishing concrete criteria for divinity, it provides both philosophical clarity and devotional focus, making it one of the most significant definitional statements in Hindu literature[1][2][15].

The verse demonstrates how ancient Sanskrit literature achieves remarkable precision in theological terminology, creating definitions that remain relevant and meaningful across millennia of spiritual inquiry and practice[10][7][14].

What are the critical editions of the Vishnu Purana?

The principal **critical edition** of the *Vishnu Purana* is:

- **The Critical Edition of the Viṣṇupurāṇam**, edited by M. M. Pathak, published in two volumes by the Oriental Institute, Vadodara (Baroda), in 1997 and 1999. This edition was prepared using a comprehensive comparison of 43 Sanskrit manuscripts, with 27 selected for establishing the text. It includes the Sanskrit text in Devanagari script and a critical apparatus documenting manuscript variants[1][2][5][6].

This Baroda edition is now considered the standard scholarly reference for the *Vishnu Purana*[1][2][5]. No other critical edition of the *Vishnu Purana* of comparable scale is known in academic circles.

Commentaries listed in this edition include those by Ratnagarbha, Nṛhari, Viṣṇuvallabhā, Gangādhara, Viṣṇucitta, and Śrīdhara[7].

A modern English translation based on this critical edition was published in 2021:  
- *The Vishnu Purana: Ancient Annals of the God with Lotus Eyes*, translated by McComas Taylor[1][5].

No other earlier “critical edition” (in the academic sense of manuscript collation and apparatus) of the *Vishnu Purana* is recognized at this level of scholarly rigor.
