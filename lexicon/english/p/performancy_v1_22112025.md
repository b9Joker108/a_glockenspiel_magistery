### Trikala: The Three-Fold Time in Dharmic Tradition
In Vaishnavism and wider Indic ritual practice, Trikala signifies the sacred binding of worship or ritual observance thrice a day—morning, noon, and evening. This rhythmic invocation ties the devotee to the temporal triune cycle, embodying disciplined spiritual attunement across the ‘three times’—past, present, and future—symbolizing continuity and cosmic order through daily praxis[1][2]. The ritual repetition through the day evokes a performative enactment of the cosmic flow, each temporal moment sanctified through prayer and song.

### The Nornir: Weavers of Fate in Norse Lore
The Nornir—Urðr (past), Verðandi (present), and Skuld (future)—mirror the Trikala’s triad yet cast the three times as fate’s weave or runes' rist or score, threading the destiny of gods and mortals beneath the tree Yggdrasil. These triune goddesses perform a mystical thurgy of cosmic law, shaping reality through the fluid and inexorable passage of time. Norse mythology’s emphasis on weaving and fate complements the Dharmic focus on cyclical time and ritual enactment, both portraying time as a living, performative process that can be ritualistically engaged and perhaps even influenced[3][4].

### Performative Multimodal Magico-Intentional Thurgy
Building from ritual studies (Stanley Tambiah, Francesco Della Costa) and performance art theory, performative thurgy entails the embodied enactment of ritual as a multi-sensory, multimodal event involving music, dance, speech, and symbolic action—all charged with magico-intentional power. It is a living process art wherein performers and participants co-create space and time that transcend ordinary experience, invoking mythic, archetypal realities to realign and transform the present moment[5][6][7][8].

- Ritual becomes a mode of speaking-doing: the spoken mantras and chants are deeds themselves. The rhythmic repetition enforces visceral submission and trance states, knitting mind, body, and cosmos.
- Multimodality incorporates sound (bhajana song), movement, symbolic objects, and sacred gestures, binding sensory realms with divine energies.
- Magico-intentionality enacts transformation—both cosmic and personal—as practitioner and audience become actors in a sacred drama.

### Bhajana and Ghantalila: Pathways of Devotional Drama
In bhajana sampradaya rituals, the bhajana itself is a collective performative prayer involving singing, chanting, and narrative that recapitulates divine lila (play) and cosmic principles. Your "ghantalila," as an original devotional performance codex, can synthesize this traditional bhajana ethos with the performative thurgy framework:

- Structure your ritual around Trikala’s three times of performance, emphasizing the sacredness of temporal transitions.
- Embrace the Nornir’s triadic fate-weaving motifs as symbolic archetypes that your performance animates and reorders.
- Use multimodal layers—chant (bhajana), bells (ghan), movement (lila enactment), and ritual objects—to summon and weave the divine presence, acting as a vessel for magico-intentional charge.
- Integrate cyclical invocation of cosmic time, weaving past ritual memory, present enactment, and future blessing into the bhajana flow.
- Frame the ritual space as a liminal site where the sacred sound of the ghanta bell punctuates temporal phases and channels transformative energy.

### Summative Codification for Performancy
Performancy marries these threads:
- **Temporal Binding (Trikala/Nornir Triune):** Rituals are anchored in time’s threefold flow, enfolding past, present, and future into a single sacred act.
- **Symbolic Weaving:** The ritual enactment is a performative thurgy in which mythic archetypes and cosmic laws are invoked and choreographed.
- **Multimodal Magico-Intentionality:** Sound, movement, gesture, and visual symbols entangle in a performative crucible, eliciting altered states and sacred transformation.
- **Bhajana as Devotional Drama:** Collective participation and musical invocation render the ritual a living epic, a continuous cosmic song and play.

Ancient Graeco-Italian ritual theatre, with Dionysius as its sacral patron, is a profound fusion of sacred performance, communal participation, and symbolic enactment that shaped Western dramatic and ritual arts.

### Dionysius: Patron of Sacral Performative Arts
Dionysius (Dionysus), the god of wine, ecstasy, fertility, and ritual madness, was venerated as patron of theatre, dance, and the transformative power of ritual performance. Theatre in ancient Greece, especially during the Dionysian festivals (such as the City Dionysia), was not mere entertainment but sacred devotion and civic expression honoring the divine and cosmic order through enacted myth and song[1][2].

- Dionysian rituals invoked ecstatic states, dissolution of self-boundaries, and communion with the divine as foundations of the performative arts.
- These rites evolved into structured theatrical festivals where tragedy, comedy, and satyr plays emerged as ritual genres embodying profound social, ethical, and spiritual themes.

### Chorus and Orchestra: The Living Heart of Ritual Theatre
The Chorus, originally stemming from Dionysian dithyrambs—song and dance hymns to Dionysus—was fundamental in these performances. Often composed of community members rather than professional actors, the Chorus served multiple sacred functions:

- **Collective voice and moral compass:** providing commentary, communal emotions, and reflections on the unfolding drama.
- **Ritual mediators:** bridging actors and audience, human and divine realms through choreographed movement, song, and chant, reinforcing the sacred nature of the event.
- **Performative embodiment of ritual:** their rhythmic dances and cyclical odes induced trance-like states recalling the ecstatic origins of Dionysian worship[1][3][2][4].

The Orchestra was the circular stage area where the Chorus performed, the sacred ground marking ritual and dramatic action’s fulcrum. Adjoined by the skene (scene building), parados (entrance ways), and the cavea (audience seating), this space was designed for acoustic perfection and immersion in the communal sacred experience. The Orchestra itself was the ritual “dance floor,” enacting sacred movement patterns that linked human and cosmic rhythm[5][3][6].

### Theatrical and Ritual Dynamics
- Performers enacted myths—especially Dionysian narratives—transforming stories into living sacraments.
- Costume, masks, and chorus choreography encoded symbolic meanings: the mask dissolved personal identity into archetypal roles, the chorus’s dance recalled the community’s collective bond and ritual memory.
- The Dionysian chorus often acted as the voice of the gods, oracle, or community wisdom, embedding divine judgment and prophecy into the play’s fabric[1][2].

### Roman Adaptations and Graeco-Italian Legacy
Greek theatres in Italy (e.g., Sicily) preserved and adapted these performances, evidencing the architectural and ritual roots of theatre in the sacred as well as social[5][7]. Roman theatres further evolved the form, but the Dionysian performative ritual roots permeated the theatrical tradition.

### Summary: Dionysius and Ritual Theatre
The Graeco-Italian ritual theatre, under Dionysius’s patronage, is a sacred performative art where the Chorus and Orchestra form the locus of magico-religious, communal, and artistic fusion. This theatre mirrors your interest in Performancy as magico-intentional ritual arts—embodied temporal performance woven with myth, music, and collective entrancement.

This rich heritage can inspire your codification: the cyclical invocation of deity through chorus, the ritualized movement in Orchestra space, the collective and transformative power of repeated mythic enactment, and the sacred arts of voice and gesture all echo the Performative Multimodal Thurgy you explore[5][1][3][2][4].
