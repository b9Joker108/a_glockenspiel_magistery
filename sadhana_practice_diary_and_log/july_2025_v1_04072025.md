**Pure perception (Tib. དག་སྣང་, dak nang, Wyl. dag snang) is there an attested Sanskrit or later Hindi analogue?**

An **attested Sanskrit analogue** for the Tibetan *དག་སྣང་* (*dak nang*, “pure perception”) is not found as a single, universally standard expression in classical Sanskrit Buddhist literature, but the core notion is well-attested through several technical terms and formulations:

**1. Closest Classical Sanskrit Terms**

**a) Pratyakṣa (प्रत्यक्ष)**
- **Meaning:** Direct perception or immediate, non-conceptual cognition
- **Significance:** In Indian Buddhist epistemology, especially Yogācāra/Vijñānavāda and as articulated by Dignāga, *pratyakṣa* is explicitly defined as “perception devoid of conceptual construction (kalpanāpodhaṃ),” meaning a cognition in its pristine state, uncontaminated by mental elaboration[1].  
- **Scholarly Usage:** “Pure perception” is sometimes glossed in contemporary scholarship and translation as *nirvikalpaka pratyakṣa* (निर्विकल्पक प्रत्यक्ष)—non-conceptual, discriminative-free perception, matching the quality sought in *dak nang*.

**b) Shuddhā-darśana (शुद्ध-दर्शन), Viśuddha-darśana (विशुद्ध-दर्शन)**
- **Meaning:** “Pure seeing” or “purified vision”
- **Usage:** Seen in Mahāyāna and Yogācāra texts to refer to a seeing or cognition that is free of kleśa (mental defilement) or conceptual overlay—parallel to Tibetan *dag snang*, especially in tantric contexts[6].

**c) Śuddha pratyaya/shuddha saṃjñā (शुद्ध प्रत्यय/शुद्ध संज्ञा)**
- **Meaning:** “Pure cognition” or “pure perception” (in the psychological and meditative sense)
- **Usage:** Occasionally in śāstric commentaries on mindfulness and dhyāna.

**2. Mahayana and Vajrayana Contexts**

- In Mahāyāna (esp. Yogācāra) and Vajrayāna Sanskrit texts, the experience of direct, unfiltered awareness (*pratyakṣa*, *śuddha-darśana*) is described as the ideal or goal of meditative purification—“seeing phenomena as empty and luminous, the union of clarity (prabhāsvara) and emptiness (śūnyatā),” closely paralleling Tibetan *dak nang*[1][5][6][7].

**3. Hindi Adaptations**

There is no widely attested colloquial Hindi technical term precisely equivalent to *dak nang*, as the rich technical vocabulary of Buddhist epistemology largely remains untranslated or borrowed from Sanskrit/Pali. Contemporary spiritual literature may use calques like:
- **“शुद्ध दृष्टि” (shuddh drishti, “pure sight”)** or
- **“निर्मल दृष्टि” (nirmal drishti, “spotless/clear sight”)**

But these are somewhat general and lack the precise doctrinal context of the Tibetan *dak nang* or the Buddhist Sanskrit terms above.

In the **Nyāya** school of Indian philosophy, the sixteen categories (padārthas) are called the "**Sixteen Topics**" (ṣoḍaśa padārtha), and among them, "Justification" or "debate" forms are included under the heading of **vāda** (discussion). ### The Sixteen Categories (Padārthas) of Nyāya

Here are the traditional sixteen topics of Nyāya:

1. **Pramāṇa** – Means of valid knowledge (perception, inference, comparison, testimony)
2. **Prameya** – Objects of valid knowledge
3. **Saṃśaya** – Doubt
4. **Prayojana** – Purpose or motive
5. **Dṛṣṭānta** – Example
6. **Siddhānta** – Conclusion or established doctrine
7. **Avayava** – Members of a syllogism
8. **Tarka** – Hypothetical reasoning
9. **Nirṇaya** – Ascertainment or determination
10. **Vāda** – Honest debate or discussion
11. **Jalpa** – Disputatious debate, aiming at victory rather than truth
12. **Vitaṇḍā** – Mere wrangling or destructive criticism, refuting the opponent without establishing one's own position
13. **Hetvābhāsa** – Fallacy
14. **Chala** – Quibble or equivocation
15. **Jāti** – Sophistical refutation, false analogies
16. **Nigrahasthāna** – Points of defeat, grounds for censure in debate

### The Three Types of Debate

Within the sixteen, three are specifically forms of debate:

- **Vāda**: Honest, truth-seeking debate
- **Jalpa**: Debate aimed at victory, often involving wrangling and eristic tactics
- **Vitaṇḍā**: Mere destructive criticism, attacking the opponent's position without defending one's own

**Jalpa** is thus the 11th topic, and it is characterized by the use of quibbles, false analogies, and attempts to win rather than to arrive at truth.

**Hypnagogic and Hypnopompic Auditory Hallucinations**

When you experience sounds—such as voices, buzzing, or loud bangs—during sleep transitions, the technical term is:

* Hypnagogic auditory hallucinations: sounds perceived as you’re falling asleep (sleep-onset).  
* Hypnopompic auditory hallucinations: sounds perceived as you’re waking up (sleep-offset).  

These phenomena fall under the broader category of hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations, which can involve any sensory modality (visual, tactile, etc.) and occur during the threshold between wakefulness and sleep. A specific subtype of hypnopompic auditory hallucinations is Exploding Head Syndrome, characterized by hearing sudden loud noises: like explosions, crashes, or cymbals, that aren’t real and often jolt the sleeper awake. Although alarming, it’s benign and usually requires no treatment beyond sleep hygiene improvements.

**Why They Occur**

1. Neural Sleep Transitions  
   During hypnagogia and hypnopompia, your brain shifts between wakeful (Alpha/Beta waves) and sleep (Theta/Delta waves) states. This asynchronous activity may generate phantom sensory experiences, including sounds.  
2. Sleep Deprivation and Stress  
   Both increase the frequency and vividness of these hallucinations.  
3. Sleep Disorders  
   Conditions like narcolepsy and insomnia often present with more hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations.
