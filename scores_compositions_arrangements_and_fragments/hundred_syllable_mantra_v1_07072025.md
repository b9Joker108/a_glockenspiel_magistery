oṃ vajrasattvasamayaṃ anupālaya vajrasattvatvenopatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhava |  
sutoṣyo me bhava supoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava |  
sarvasiddhiṃ me prayaccha sarvakarmasuca me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ |  
ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavan sarvatathāgatavajra mā me muñca vajrī bhava mahāsamayasattva āḥ ||

## 1. Grammatical Analysis

### Line 1:  
**oṃ vajrasattvasamayaṃ anupālaya vajrasattvatvenopatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhava**

- **oṃ**: Pranava, sacred syllable.
- **vajrasattvasamayaṃ**:  
  - *vajrasattva* (compound noun, “Diamond Being” or “Vajra Being”) + *samayaṃ* (accusative singular of *samaya*, “pledge, vow”)  
  - “the samaya of Vajrasattva”
- **anupālaya**:  
  - Imperative verb, 2nd person singular of *anupālayati* (“to protect, maintain”)  
  - “Protect, uphold”
- **vajrasattvatvena**:  
  - Instrumental singular of *vajrasattva* + *-tvena* (suffix meaning “as, like”)  
  - “as Vajrasattva”
- **upatiṣṭha**:  
  - Imperative verb, 2nd person singular of *upatiṣṭhati* (“to stand near, abide”)  
  - “Abide, remain near”
- **dṛḍho me bhava**:  
  - *dṛḍhaḥ* (adjective, “firm, strong”) + *me* (1st person singular possessive pronoun, “my”) + *bhava* (imperative of *bhū*, “be”)  
  - “Be firm for me”

### Line 2:  
**sutoṣyo me bhava supoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava**

- Three parallel imperatives:  
  - *sutoṣya* (from *su-toṣa*, “well-satisfied”)  
  - *supoṣya* (from *su-poṣa*, “well-nourished”)  
  - *anurakta* (“attached, loving”)  
- Each followed by *me bhava*: “be to me”  
- Meaning: “Be well-satisfied to me, be well-nourished to me, be loving to me.”

### Line 3:  
**sarvasiddhiṃ me prayaccha sarvakarmasuca me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ**

- **sarvasiddhiṃ me prayaccha**:  
  - *sarva-siddhiṃ*: “all accomplishments” (accusative singular)  
  - *me*: “to me”  
  - *prayaccha*: imperative of *pradā* (“give, grant”)  
  - “Grant me all accomplishments”
- **sarvakarmasuca me**:  
  - *sarva-karma*: “all actions”  
  - *suca*: imperative verb “purify” (mantraic form of *śuc*)  
  - *me*: “my”  
  - “Purify all my karma”
- **cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru**:  
  - *cittaṃ*: “mind” (accusative singular)  
  - *śreyaḥ*: “good, auspicious, virtuous” (accusative singular)  
  - *kuru*: imperative of *karoti* (“do, make”)  
  - “Make my mind virtuous”
- **hūṃ**: Seed syllable (bīja mantra), representing the essence of purification and protection.

### Line 4:  
**ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavan sarvatathāgatavajra mā me muñca vajrī bhava mahāsamayasattva āḥ**

- **ha ha ha ha hoḥ**:  
  - Four exclamations “ha” + “hoḥ”, a mantraic expression of joy, power, or invocation.
- **bhagavan**:  
  - Vocative of *bhagavān*, “Blessed One, Lord”
- **sarvatathāgatavajra**:  
  - Compound of *sarva* (all) + *tathāgata* (thus-gone one, Buddha) + *vajra* (diamond, thunderbolt)  
  - “Vajra of all Tathāgatas”
- **mā me muñca**:  
  - *mā*: negative imperative particle (“do not”)  
  - *me*: “me”  
  - *muñca*: imperative of *muc* (“release, abandon”)  
  - “Do not abandon me”
- **vajrī bhava**:  
  - *vajrī*: “vajra-holder, diamond-like one” (imperative)  
  - *bhava*: “be” (imperative)  
  - “Be a vajra-holder”
- **mahāsamayasattva**:  
  - Compound noun: *mahā* (great) + *samaya* (vow, commitment) + *sattva* (being)  
  - “Great Samaya Being” (epithet of Vajrasattva)
- **āḥ**:  
  - Seed syllable, closing mantra sound.

## 3. Semantic Analysis

- The mantra is a **devotional and ritual plea** to Vajrasattva, the embodiment of purity and the remover of obscurations.
- The **first line** requests Vajrasattva to uphold his sacred vow and remain firmly present.
- The **second line** asks for satisfaction, nourishment, and loving connection—spiritual sustenance.
- The **third line** requests comprehensive spiritual accomplishments, purification of all karma, and the transformation of the mind into virtue.
- The **final line** is a joyful invocation, imploring Vajrasattva not to abandon the practitioner and to empower them as a vajra-holder, affirming Vajrasattva’s identity as the great samaya-being.

## 4. Syntagmatic and Pragmatic Analysis

- The mantra is structured as a **series of imperatives and vocatives**, forming a ritual dialogue between the practitioner and Vajrasattva.
- The **imperative verbs** (anupālaya, upatiṣṭha, bhava, prayaccha, suca, kuru, muñca, bhava) create a dynamic flow of requests and commands.
- The **repetition of “me bhava”** (“be to me”) emphasizes the personal and intimate nature of the supplication.
- The **seed syllables (hūṃ, ha, hoḥ, āḥ)** punctuate the mantra, serving as sonic seals that enhance ritual efficacy and spiritual power.

## 5. Philological Observations

- The mantra preserves **classical Sanskrit vocabulary** but also exhibits **mantraic phonetic forms** (e.g., “suca” instead of “śuci” or “śodhaya”).
- The **compound formations** (e.g., sarvakarmasuca, sarvatathāgatavajra) are typical of tantric Sanskrit, where **condensed compounds convey dense theological meaning**.
- The **mantra’s transmission through Tibetan and other languages** has preserved its phonetic integrity, though orthographic variations exist.
- The **seed syllables** (bīja) are non-lexical but carry profound ritual and symbolic import, often linked to specific energies or deities.

## **Philological and Mantraic Notes**

- **Mantraic Compression:**  
  Many words are **syntactically compressed** or use **phonetic forms** for euphony and ritual power, sometimes deviating from classical Sanskrit grammar[1][2].
- **Seed Syllables (bīja):**  
  oṃ, hūṃ, āḥ, ha, hoḥ are **ritual sounds** with layered symbolic meanings, not subject to normal morphological analysis.
- **Vocatives and Imperatives:**  
  The mantra is rich in **vocative and imperative forms**, reflecting its nature as a direct invocation and supplication to Vajrasattva.
- **Compound Nouns:**  
  Sanskrit’s ability to form long compounds is used to create dense, theologically meaningful epithets (e.g., sarvatathāgatavajra, mahāsamayasattva).

This designation is a back-formation based on descriptive conventions, not an attested title in Sanskrit scriptures or early Indian commentaries.

### What is attested?

- **In Sanskrit manuscripts and Indian tantras**, the mantra is typically presented without a formalized title. The text is often introduced contextually, e.g., “Now, the hundred-syllable mantra of Vajrasattva is as follows…” rather than with a fixed name.
- **Tibetan tradition** refers to it as ཡིག་བརྒྱ་ (*yig brgya*, “hundred syllables”), a descriptive label that became standard in Tibetan ritual and commentary literature.
- Modern Sanskritized references such as “शताक्षरीमन्त्रः” (śatākṣarīmantraḥ, “hundred-syllable mantra”) or “वज्रसत्त्वशताक्षरीमन्त्रः” are **not found in early Sanskrit sources** but are used in contemporary liturgical and academic contexts for clarity and convenience.

### Scholarly consensus

- **Indological and Buddhist studies scholarship** (see, for example, Ronald Davidson’s work on Indian Vajrayāna, or the translations and commentaries of the *Vajrasekhara* and *Guhyasamāja* tantras) does not cite a fixed Sanskrit title for this mantra.
- **Tibetan ritual manuals and translations** (such as the *Guhyasamāja* and *Vajrasattva* sādhanā texts) consistently use the Tibetan descriptive term and sometimes provide a Sanskritized equivalent for clarity, but this is a later development.

### Conclusion

**There is no classical Sanskrit manuscript or tantra that gives the mantra a formalized title such as “शताक्षरी वज्रसत्त्वमन्त्रः.”** The name is a modern or Tibetan-derived descriptive label, not a canonical Sanskrit title.
