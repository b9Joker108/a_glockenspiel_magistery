## **Philological and Mantraic Notes**

- **Mantraic Compression:**  
  Many words are **syntactically compressed** or use **phonetic forms** for euphony and ritual power, sometimes deviating from classical Sanskrit grammar[1][2].
- **Seed Syllables (bīja):**  
  oṃ, hūṃ, āḥ, ha, hoḥ are **ritual sounds** with layered symbolic meanings, not subject to normal morphological analysis.
- **Vocatives and Imperatives:**  
  The mantra is rich in **vocative and imperative forms**, reflecting its nature as a direct invocation and supplication to Vajrasattva.
- **Compound Nouns:**  
  Sanskrit’s ability to form long compounds is used to create dense, theologically meaningful epithets (e.g., sarvatathāgatavajra, mahāsamayasattva).

**शताक्षरी वज्रसत्त्वमन्त्रः**  
**śatākṣarī vajrasattva mantraḥ**

This means **“The Hundred Syllable Vajrasattva Mantra”**.

You may also see it simply as:

**वज्रसत्त्वशताक्षरीमन्त्रः**  
**vajrasattvaśatākṣarīmantraḥ**

Or, more simply:

**वज्रसत्त्वमन्त्रः**  
**vajrasattva mantraḥ**

In Tibetan Buddhist sources, it is called:

ཡིག་བརྒྱ་ (yig brgya, “hundred syllables”)  
Wylie: yig brgya

- **Devanagari:** शताक्षरी वज्रसत्त्वमन्त्रः

- **EWTS:** yig brgya  
- **English:** Hundred Syllable Vajrasattva Mantra

This is the standard, scriptural, and liturgical name for the mantra in Sanskritic and Tibetan traditions[1][2][5][6].

What is the earliest extant attested form? In what text?

The **earliest extant attested form** of the Hundred Syllable Vajrasattva Mantra is found in the **Sarvatathāgata-tattvasaṃgraha** (STTS), a foundational Yoga Tantra text from India, dating to the late 7th or early 8th century. The **oldest datable literary occurrence** is in the Chinese Buddhist canon, specifically in **Taisho 866** (T.866), a collection of mantras related to the STTS, translated into Chinese by **Vajrabodhi** in 723 CE[2][5][7].

### Details and Sources

- **Textual Source:**  
  The *Sarvatathāgata-tattvasaṃgraha* (STTS), an Indian Yoga Tantra, is the parent text.  
  The mantra appears in a collection of mantras (T.866: 金剛頂瑜伽中略出念誦經) translated into Chinese by Vajrabodhi (671–741) in 723 CE[2][7].

- **Sanskrit Manuscripts:**  
  The oldest surviving Sanskrit manuscript is a Nepalese palm-leaf manuscript (10th century), written in Siddhaṃ script, published in facsimile by Candra and Snellgrove[5].  
  Critical editions (e.g., by Yamada) are based on these and other later Sanskrit manuscripts, but the **earliest datable evidence** is the Chinese translation.

- **Scholarly Summary:**  
  - The mantra is **not found in earlier Mahayana sutras** or the earliest Vajrayana texts.
  - Its first clear, complete, and datable appearance is in the **Chinese translation of the STTS-related mantra collection (T.866, 723 CE)**[2][7].
  - The Sanskrit text in the STTS manuscript tradition matches the form familiar in Tibetan and later Sanskrit recensions, with only minor orthographic and phonetic variations[5][7].

### **Fertility and Fecundity in Vedic and Post-Vedic Traditions**

#### **Rig Veda and Early Indian Religion**
- **Primary Sources:**  
  - *Rig Veda*: See Stephanie Jamison & Joel Brereton, *The Rigveda: The Earliest Religious Poetry of India* (Oxford, 2014). Hymns to Prithvi, Aditi, Ushas, and Indra are rich in agricultural, sexual, and cosmic fertility imagery.
  - *Atharva Veda*: Contains explicit hymns for fertility of fields, cattle, and women (see Laurie Patton, *Bringing the Gods to Mind*, 2005).
- **Secondary Literature:**  
  - Wendy Doniger, *The Hindus: An Alternative History* (Penguin, 2009) — especially chapters on Vedic ritual and sexuality.
  - David Kinsley, *Hindu Goddesses* (University of California Press, 1986) — explores the goddess as a source of fertility and abundance.
  - Alf Hiltebeitel, *The Cult of Draupadi* (University of Chicago Press, 1988) — discusses the persistence of fertility cults in South India.

#### **Transformation in Medieval India**
- **Dharmashastra and Ascetic Movements:**  
  - Patrick Olivelle, *Manu’s Code of Law: A Critical Edition and Translation of the Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* (OUP, 2005) — shows increasing emphasis on purity, sexual restraint, and social order.
  - Gavin Flood, *An Introduction to Hinduism* (Cambridge, 1996) — details the rise of renunciation and the tension with earlier fertility cults.
- **Bhakti and Tantric Traditions:**  
  - June McDaniel, *Offering Flowers, Feeding Skulls: Popular Goddess Worship in West Bengal* (OUP, 2004) — shows how fertility themes persist in Tantric and folk practice, often outside mainstream reformist discourse.
  - Hugh Urban, *The Power of Tantra: Religion, Sexuality, and the Politics of South Asian Studies* (I.B. Tauris, 2010) — discusses how Tantra preserved and reinterpreted fertility and sexuality.
