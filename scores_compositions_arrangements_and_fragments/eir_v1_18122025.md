**Eir Valkyrie runic inscription from Bergen, Norway from around 1300**

The Bergen Eir inscription is a brief runic text on a wooden stick from Bryggen, Bergen, dated to around 1300, in which the divine name *Eir* appears as part of a poetic kenning for “woman.”[2][4] It is one of the very few non-literary attestations tying Eir—otherwise known from the Eddas and skaldic verse—to lived, written practice in medieval Norway.[2][3]

## Context and dating

The stick belongs to the large corpus of Bryggen runic inscriptions, everyday texts (labels, messages, charms, verses) written in runes on wood and bone in a mercantile-urban milieu.[4][5] Paleography and archaeological context place this particular inscription to about 1300, when Bergen was a major Hanseatic and Norwegian trading hub and runes still functioned as a practical script alongside Latin letters.[4][5]

## The text and kenning

The stick first records a routine commercial transaction, then shifts into a short verse in the skaldic style, where *Eir* appears in a woman-kenning.[2][4] In translation, the relevant part reads approximately: “Wise Várr of wire [‘woman of filigree’] makes me sit unhappy; Eir of the mackerels’ ground often takes much sleep from me,” where “wire/filigree” and “mackerels’ ground” are metaphors pointing to gold and thus to a richly adorned woman.[2][4]

## Eir’s role and interpretation

In this kenning, *Eir* functions as a base word for “woman,” paralleling skaldic usage where valkyrie or goddess names form the core of female kennings.[2] The inscription therefore supports reading Eir as a mythic female figure known well enough in 13th–14th‑century Bergen to anchor a love-lament about an adorned woman who robs the scribe of sleep.[2][4] This usage is consistent with broader traditions that present Eir as either a healing goddess or a valkyrie associated with care, but the inscription itself is strictly lexical-poetic rather than cultic.[1][2]

## Significance for Eir and Valkyries

Philologically, the Bergen stick shows that Eir’s name circulated outside learned literary contexts and was productive in everyday verse composition.[2][4] Religio-historically, it illustrates how mythic female beings (valkyries, goddesses, helper-maidens) were embedded in late-medieval urban discourse as poetic resources, even in what is otherwise a mundane business document.[1][4] Scholars use this inscription, together with the Eddic and skaldic references, to debate whether Eir is best classified as an ásynja, a healing valkyrie, or a helper of Menglöð; the Bergen attestation cannot resolve this, but confirms her ongoing cultural salience around 1300.[1][2][3]
